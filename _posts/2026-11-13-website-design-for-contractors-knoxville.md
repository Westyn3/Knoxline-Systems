## A Contractor Website Has One Job

Most business websites try to do too many things at once.

A contractor website has one main job: turn a stranger with a problem into a phone call or a form submission.

Everything on the site should support that job.

Your logo matters a little. Your color scheme matters a little. Your "about us" story matters some.

But the homeowner standing in a wet basement is asking four questions, and they want the answers in a few seconds:

- **Do you do the thing I need?**
- **Do you work where I live?**
- **Can I trust you?**
- **How do I reach you right now?**

If your site answers those four questions quickly on a phone, you are ahead of a lot of contractor websites in the Knoxville area.

If it doesn't, it does not matter how nice it looks.

## The Must-Have Elements Above the Fold

"Above the fold" just means what someone sees on their phone before they scroll. That is your most valuable space, and it is where many contractor sites waste the most.

Here is what we want to see there.

### A headline that says what you do and where

Not "Quality You Can Trust." Not "Building Dreams Since 2009."

Something like:

**"Roof Repair and Replacement in Knoxville and East Tennessee"**

**"Licensed Plumbers Serving Knoxville, Farragut and Maryville"**

**"Handyman Services for Knoxville Homeowners"**

That kind of headline does two jobs. It tells the visitor they are in the right place, and it tells Google what the page is about.

### A phone number you can tap

On a phone, the number should be a tap-to-call link. One tap, and the phone dials.

It should be in the header, and it should stay easy to find as someone scrolls.

This sounds basic. It is basic. And we still regularly see contractor sites where the phone number is a plain image, or sits only in the footer, or requires a trip to the contact page.

### One clear next step

A "Call Now" button and a "Request an Estimate" button are plenty.

You don't need six buttons fighting for attention.

### A trust signal

Something that makes a stranger feel safer. That might be your star rating and review count from Google, a line about being licensed and insured (only if you are), or how long you have been in business.

Use real information only. A homeowner can check your Google reviews in about five seconds, so the claims on your site need to match what they find.

## Page Structure: How a Contractor Site Should Be Organized

Once the homepage does its job, the rest of the site needs a structure that makes sense to both people and search engines.

For most home-service businesses, we build around something like this:

1. **Homepage.** Who you are, what you do, where you work, how to contact you.
2. **One page per main service.** Each service gets its own page.
3. **Service area pages.** Useful pages for the main communities you serve.
4. **Work or gallery page.** Real photos of real jobs.
5. **About page.** The people behind the company.
6. **Contact page.** Phone, form, hours, service area.
7. **Blog.** Answers to the questions customers actually ask.

That is not a huge site. But each page has a clear reason to exist.

## Why Every Service Needs Its Own Page

This is one of the biggest differences between contractor websites that get found and ones that don't.

A lot of sites have a single "Services" page with a bulleted list:

- Roof repair
- Roof replacement
- Gutters
- Inspections
- Storm damage

That list tells a visitor what you do. But it gives Google very little to work with when someone searches "roof leak repair Knoxville."

A dedicated page for roof repair can explain:

- the types of repairs you handle,
- what the process looks like from the first call to the finished job,
- signs a homeowner should look for,
- whether you work with insurance claims (if you do),
- the areas you serve for that work,
- common questions and honest answers,
- and a clear way to call or request an estimate.

Now the page has real substance. It answers the question the homeowner was asking when they searched.

### What this looks like by trade

**For plumbers:** separate pages for drain cleaning, water heater repair and replacement, leak detection, sewer line work, fixture installs and emergency plumbing, if those are services you actually offer. Website design for plumbers in Knoxville should start from the calls you want most, then build pages around them.

**For handymen:** handyman work is broad, so structure matters even more. Group the work into categories homeowners recognize: drywall repair, door and trim work, small carpentry, fixture swaps, deck repair, punch lists for home sales. Website design for a handyman in Knoxville works best when a homeowner can quickly see "yes, they do that kind of job."

**For general contractors and remodelers:** project-type pages such as kitchen remodels, bathroom remodels, additions and basement finishing, each with real project photos.

The rule is the same everywhere. **If it is a service you want calls for, it deserves its own page.**

## Service Area Pages, Done Honestly

Most contractors don't only work in one city. A Knoxville plumber may cover Farragut, Maryville, Oak Ridge, Powell, Lenoir City and more.

Service area pages can help a homeowner in those places see that you actually come to them.

But there is a wrong way to do this, and it is very common.

The wrong way is a stack of pages that are identical except for the city name. "Best Plumber in Maryville." "Best Plumber in Oak Ridge." Same paragraphs, same photos, one word swapped.

Google's guidelines warn against pages like that, often called doorway pages, and homeowners can tell when they are reading filler.

The better approach:

- Build pages only for the areas you genuinely serve and want work in.
- Write something specific to each area: the services you most often do there, how you handle scheduling there, real projects you have completed there (with the customer's permission).
- Link each area page to the relevant service pages.
- Keep the list of areas consistent with the service areas on your Google Business Profile.

We use this approach across our own work. Our realtor clients' sites, for example, have dedicated community and area pages that are each written for that place, which we covered in [our post on hand-built versus template websites](/blog/hand-built-vs-template-websites-realtor-local-seo/).

## Calls and Forms: Where Most Sites Quietly Fail

A beautiful website that does not reliably deliver leads is just an expensive brochure.

Here is where we see contractor sites lose leads without anyone noticing.

### The form goes nowhere

The form was set up years ago and sends to an email address nobody checks anymore. Or it lands in spam. Or it silently fails.

**Test your form this week.** Fill it out on your phone like a customer would. See where it goes and how long it takes to arrive.

### The form asks too much

A homeowner with a leaking roof does not want to fill out fifteen fields.

Name, phone, email, address or ZIP code, and a short description of the problem is usually enough. You can get the rest on the phone.

### No confirmation

After someone submits, they should see a clear message that it went through and what happens next. "Thanks, we'll call you within one business day" is better than a blank screen.

### No way to measure

If you don't know how many calls and form submissions came from the website, you can't tell whether it is working. We want every site set up so calls and form submissions can actually be counted, and we send monthly ranking reports from Search Console so you can see how the site is doing in Google.

This connects to a lesson we wrote about in our [Google Ads ghost conversions post](/blog/are-your-google-ads-leads-real-how-we-found-40-ghost-conversions-in-a-home-service-ad-account/): if you are going to measure leads, make sure you are measuring real ones.

## Speed: The Part Owners Never See

Most owners look at their own website on a fast office connection or on Wi-Fi at home. It seems fine.

Your customers are on a phone, maybe on a weak signal, maybe standing in a yard.

A slow site loses people before they ever read a word.

This is one reason we hand-build contractor websites in plain HTML and CSS instead of using a heavy page builder. There is less code for the phone to download and process, and we control exactly what loads and when.

We hold ourselves to this on our own site, too.

When we tested knoxlinesystems.com in October 2026, Google PageSpeed scored mobile performance at 76. Not terrible, but not where we wanted it. The biggest problem was about 2.1 seconds of render-blocking requests, mostly from loading Google Fonts.

The fixes were practical:

- **Self-hosting our fonts** and preloading them, instead of pulling them from another server.
- **Loading analytics after the page** instead of before it.
- **Making the main headline paint immediately** so visitors see something useful right away.

None of that is glamorous. All of it matters.

On the SEO side, the same site scored 100 in Google Lighthouse's SEO audit on every page we tested. That score checks technical basics like titles, descriptions, crawlability and mobile-friendly setup. It is not a ranking guarantee, but it means nothing basic is getting in the way.

## Metadata: The Words Google Shows Before Anyone Clicks

Every page on your site has a title tag and a meta description. Most homeowners never think about them, but they see them every time they search: the title is usually the blue headline in Google results, and the description often shows underneath.

On a lot of contractor sites, every page has the same title, something like "Home | Smith Plumbing."

That wastes a chance to tell Google and the searcher what each page is about.

Better examples:

- "Water Heater Repair in Knoxville, TN | Smith Plumbing"
- "Drywall Repair and Handyman Services in Knoxville"
- "Roof Replacement in Maryville and Blount County"

Each page gets its own title and its own description, written for a human, with the service and area included naturally.

We made a small but useful change on our own site in October 2026: we added "Knoxville-based, serving the Southeast" to our titles instead of positioning ourselves as Knoxville-only. It is accurate, since we work with businesses across the Southeast, and it lets our pages be relevant to more of the searches we actually want.

Hand-built sites make this easy. We control every title, description and piece of structured data directly, instead of working around what a template allows.

## Match Your Website to Your Google Business Profile

For a home-service company, your Google Business Profile is often the first thing a homeowner sees. Your website is usually the second.

They need to agree.

Your business name, phone number, hours, service areas and services should be the same on both. Not close. The same.

When we updated our own site, we matched it to our Google Business Profile exactly: legal business name, phone, hours, the same 20 service areas listed on the profile, and services that line up with our profile categories. We will go deeper on why this matters in an upcoming post, but the short version is simple.

**Consistency helps Google be confident you are exactly who you say you are.**

## What Results Actually Look Like (and How Long They Take)

Here is the honest part.

A new website does not rank overnight. Organic SEO usually takes months, and around six months is a fair expectation before you judge it. Anyone promising you page one by next month should make you nervous.

What you usually see first is movement in Google Search Console: more impressions (how often you appear in results) and better average positions. Clicks and calls follow once you reach the first page for searches people actually use.

Our own site is a good example. After our October 2026 changes, Search Console showed:

- Impressions going from about 6 per day to about 29 per day in the first week.
- The search "website design for contractor knoxville" moving from an average position around 43 to about 10–11.
- "knoxville google ads" moving from an average position of 54 (on a three-month view) to about 20 within roughly three weeks.

And clicks were still near zero at that point.

That is normal. **Rankings move first. Clicks follow when you reach page one.** That is why we report on rankings and impressions monthly, so you can see the progress before the phone starts ringing more.

## Common Contractor Website Mistakes We See

A quick checklist. If any of these sound familiar, they are worth fixing.

- The phone number is not tap-to-call on mobile.
- All services are crammed onto one page.
- The service area is never clearly stated.
- City pages are copy-paste with the name swapped.
- Every page has the same title tag.
- Stock photos of people who obviously don't work for you.
- A contact form nobody has tested in a year.
- Hours on the website don't match hours on Google.
- The site was built years ago and never updated.
- Nobody knows how many leads it produces.

None of these require a giant budget to fix. They require someone paying attention.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a website if I already have a Google Business Profile?

In our view, yes. Your profile is important, but it is limited in what it can explain. A website lets you describe each service in depth, show your work, answer common questions and give Google more to understand about your business. The two work best together.

### How long does it take for a new contractor website to show up on Google?

It varies by market, competition and the condition of your current site. Google can find and index a new site fairly quickly, but earning strong positions for competitive searches usually takes months. We tell clients to expect around six months before judging organic results, and we share monthly Search Console reports along the way.

### Can I just use Wix or Squarespace?

You can, and for some businesses it is a reasonable start. The tradeoff is control. Hand-built HTML and CSS sites give us more control over speed, metadata and structured data than most website builders and WordPress themes. For a contractor competing in local search, that control is worth a lot.
