Search "plumber near me" on your phone.

Before you see a single website, you'll usually see a map. A few pins. A short list of businesses with star ratings, review counts, and a button to call.

That's the map pack.

For a lot of home-service businesses, it's where the call gets won or lost. A homeowner with water on the floor often doesn't scroll any further. They look at the businesses on the map, glance at the ratings, and tap call.

**If you're not in that short list, you may not be in the conversation at all.**

This post explains how the map pack works, using Google's own stated framework, and what a home-service business in Knoxville or anywhere in the Southeast can do to climb it. No tricks. No guarantees. Just how the system works and how to build a profile that deserves to be there.

## What the Map Pack Is

When someone searches for a local service, Google often shows a block of local business results tied to a map. These results come from Google Business Profiles, not from websites directly.

That's an important distinction.

Your website helps. But the listing in the map pack is your Google Business Profile. If the profile is incomplete, inaccurate, or not verified, your website alone can't put you there.

Tapping into the map results or opening Google Maps itself shows a longer list. But the first few businesses get the most attention, because they're the ones people see without scrolling.

## How Google Decides: Relevance, Distance and Prominence

Google has been open about the three main factors it uses to rank local results. It says local results are based mainly on **relevance, distance and prominence**.

Here's what each one means in practice.

### Relevance

Relevance is how well your profile matches what someone searched for.

If someone searches "septic pumping," Google is looking for businesses that clearly do septic pumping. A profile with the right category, listed services and a clear description is easier for Google to match than one with vague information.

Relevance is where most businesses have the most room to improve, because it's largely in their control.

### Distance

Distance is how far each business is from the location in the search. If the search includes a place, like "roofer Maryville," Google uses that. If it doesn't, Google uses what it knows about the searcher's location.

You can't change where a customer is standing when they search. That's why distance is the factor you have the least control over, and why it's normal to show up strongly close to your base and less strongly further out.

### Prominence

Prominence is how well-known and well-regarded your business is. Google says this is based on information it has about a business from across the web, like links, articles and directories. Google review count and rating are part of it. Google also says a business's position in regular web search results is a factor, which is one reason a strong website matters for map results too.

Prominence is built over time. It's the slowest of the three to change, and the hardest for a competitor to copy.

## Start With the Basics: A Verified, Accurate Profile

Before anything else, the profile needs to exist, be verified and be accurate.

- **The business name must be your real business name.** Don't add keywords or towns. "Smith Plumbing" should not become "Smith Plumbing Emergency Plumber Knoxville TN." Keyword stuffing is against Google's guidelines and can lead to the profile being suspended.
- **Google only allows profiles for businesses that make in-person contact with customers.** Service-area businesses that go to the customer can hide their address.
- **Verification is required.** Depending on the business, Google may ask for video verification. If you've had trouble getting verified, we covered common reasons and how to prepare in [Google Denied Your Business Profile? ## Categories: The Biggest Relevance Lever

Your primary category tells Google what kind of business you are. It's one of the most important choices on the profile.

### Pick the primary category carefully

Choose the category that best describes your core business. A company that mainly does septic work should probably pick a septic-related category as primary, even if it also does grading. A company that mainly does roofing should pick a roofing category, even if it also does gutters.

### Add accurate secondary categories

Secondary categories can describe other real parts of your business. Only add categories for work you actually do. Adding unrelated categories doesn't make you more relevant. It makes the profile less clear.

### An example from our own profile

For our own business, our primary category is Business management consultant, with secondary categories of Website designer, Automation company, Marketing consultant, Internet marketing service and Business development service. Each one matches work we actually do, and the same services are reflected on our website.

## Services and Products

### Services

The services section lets you list the specific services you offer. This is a direct way to help with relevance.

List the services the way customers search for them. "Septic tank pumping." "Drain field repair." "Roof replacement." "AC repair." Keep them aligned with the service pages on your website.

### Products

Depending on your business type, your profile may also have a products section. Some home-service businesses use it to highlight specific packages or common jobs, with a photo and a short description. It's optional, but it gives the profile more useful detail and gives customers something to look at.

Only list what you actually offer. Don't use products as a place to stuff keywords.

## Posts

Google Business Profile lets you publish updates (often called posts) that appear on your profile.

Posts can share:

- Seasonal reminders (septic pumping before the holidays, AC tune-ups before summer).
- Recent projects, with photos.
- Offers, if you run them.
- News about the business.

We're careful about how we describe posts. They're a way to keep the profile current and give customers useful information. We wouldn't promise that posting alone moves rankings. But a profile that's clearly active and up to date gives customers more confidence.

## Photos

Photos are often the first impression a homeowner gets of your business on the map.

Good photos for a home-service business include:

- Your trucks and equipment, with your branding visible.
- Your crew at work.
- Before-and-after photos from real jobs.
- Your office or shop, if customers visit it.
- Your logo and a clear cover photo.

Use your own photos, not stock images. Add new ones over time, not all at once and then nothing for two years.

## Reviews: The Most Visible Part of Prominence

Reviews are the part of the profile customers look at first. Star rating and review count appear right in the map results.

They also connect directly to prominence. Google says review count and rating factor into local ranking.

### Build a steady process

The businesses with strong review profiles usually aren't lucky. They ask every customer, consistently, at the right time. A direct review link, QR code cards for technicians to hand out, and a follow-up text after the job make it routine.

### Reply to every review

Replying shows future customers how you treat people. It also shows that the profile is actively managed.

### Stay within Google's rules

Never buy reviews, never offer incentives for reviews, never ask only happy customers, and never trade reviews with other businesses. Those practices are against Google's policies and can put your profile at risk.

## Service Area Strategy: Start Where You Show Up

Here's where a lot of businesses go wrong.

They list every county within two hours on the profile, then wonder why they don't show up in most of them.

Remember distance. Google weighs how close you are to the searcher. A business based in Knoxville will naturally have an easier time showing up in Knoxville, Farragut and Karns than in a town 90 minutes away.

That doesn't mean you can't win work further out. It means you should be strategic.

### Step 1: List the areas you actually serve

Your service areas should reflect where you really work. On our own profile, we list 20 service areas, and our website lists the same 20.

### Step 2: Find where you already show up

Search your main services from different parts of your service area, or have someone else do it. Note where you appear in the map pack and where you don't.

### Step 3: Win close to home first

Strengthen your position where you're already competitive. More reviews, better photos, accurate categories and a stronger website all help prominence and relevance everywhere, but you'll usually see it first near your base.

### Step 4: Expand as you win

As your profile gets stronger, you can push into nearby towns. Real service area pages on your website, real projects in those towns, and reviews from customers there all help make the case. Those pages need real local content, not the same text with a new town name pasted in.

### Don't fake locations

Don't create profiles at addresses where you don't operate. Virtual offices and fake addresses violate Google's guidelines and can get profiles removed.

## Your Website Still Matters for the Map

Even though the map pack shows profiles, your website plays a real role.

- **It supports prominence.** Google says position in web results is a factor.
- **It confirms your information.** When your website matches your profile exactly (name, phone, hours, services, service areas), it's easier for Google to trust both. We explained why in [Why Your Website Should Match Your Google Business Profile 100%](/blog/match-website-to-google-business-profile/).
- **It's where people go next.** Many customers tap "Website" on the profile before calling. A slow, confusing site loses them.

Structured data helps here too. On our own site, our business schema includes a sameAs link to our permanent Google Maps listing, which helps connect the website and the profile.

## What to Expect

Google Maps marketing is not instant.

Improving relevance (categories, services, description) can be done quickly. Building prominence (reviews, links, web rankings) takes months. Organic local SEO, including map results, usually takes months to show clear movement, and around six months is a fair expectation.

Nobody can guarantee a spot in the map pack. Results vary by search, location and device, and Google changes how it displays results. Anyone who promises a guaranteed top-three spot should make you cautious.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why do I show up in the map pack at my office but not across town?

Distance is one of Google's three main factors. You'll usually be strongest near your business location and weaker further out. Building reviews, relevance and website strength can help you compete further away over time.

### Do I need a physical storefront to appear on Google Maps?

No. Service-area businesses that visit customers can have a profile and hide their address. Google does require that the business make in-person contact with customers.

### Will adding my city to my business name help?

It might seem to help briefly, but it's against Google's guidelines unless it's truly your legal business name. It can lead to suspension. Use your real name.

### How often should I post on my Google Business Profile?

There's no required schedule. Post when you have something useful: a seasonal reminder, a recent project, an update. Consistency matters more than frequency.
