Most local businesses don't own a website built specifically for their business.

They rent a template.

That's especially common in real estate.

A website provider offers a prebuilt design, swaps in the agent's headshot, changes a few colors, connects an MLS feed, and publishes it.

Technically, the agent now has a website.

But there's a problem:

\*\*Hundreds or thousands of other agents may have essentially the same one.\*\*

When we built websites for North Georgia realtors Britney Javens and Karen Hilliard, we took a different approach.

Instead of starting with a real-estate template, we started with their actual businesses.

Where do they sell?

What types of properties do they specialize in?

What are potential buyers searching for?

What should happen when someone lands on the website?

Where should an MLS search send the lead?

How does the website connect to their Google presence and existing reputation?

Then we built around those answers.

The result was two fast, hand-built websites with dedicated local-market content, integrated property search, stronger connections to their Google Business Profiles and reviews, and \*\*100/100 Google Lighthouse SEO scores\*\* at testing.

But the score isn't the important part.

The important part is what the website is designed to do:

\*\*Help the right customer find the business, trust the business, and contact the business.\*\*

## What Does "Hand-Built Website" Actually Mean?

A hand-built website doesn't mean we refuse to use tools or libraries.

It means the site isn't being forced into a generic template designed to work for thousands of unrelated businesses.

The structure is built around the actual company.

For a realtor, that might mean creating dedicated content around the individual communities they serve.

For a roofing company, it might mean building around roof replacement, repair, storm damage, and its service area.

For a septic company, it could mean septic pumping, installation, repair, and the specific communities it serves.

The website architecture follows the business.

Not the other way around.

## Why Template Websites Are So Common

Template platforms exist for a reason.

They're fast to launch.

They're relatively inexpensive.

They can give a business a professional-looking web presence without custom development.

And for some businesses, that's perfectly adequate.

The problem starts when a business expects a generic template to function as a serious local-search and lead-generation asset without doing the work beyond the template.

A beautiful homepage isn't automatically an SEO strategy.

A website still needs to communicate:

\*\*What does this business do?\*\*

\*\*Where does it operate?\*\*

\*\*Why should someone trust it?\*\*

\*\*What should a visitor do next?\*\*

If every important answer is buried inside a generic five-page site, the business may be leaving a lot of opportunity on the table.

## Realtor Websites Are a Perfect Example

Real estate is extremely local.

Someone doesn't usually search:

\*\*"house."\*\*

They search for a place.

They may search for:

\*\*homes for sale in Alpharetta, Georgia\*\*

\*\*homes for sale in Cumming, Georgia\*\*

\*\*Lake Lanier waterfront homes\*\*

\*\*Blue Ridge cabins for sale\*\*

\*\*North Atlanta real estate\*\*

Those searches contain intent.

The buyer is telling Google exactly what market they're interested in.

So why would a realtor's website treat every market as if it were the same?

That's one of the first things we wanted to address with Britney and Karen's websites.

## Building Britney Javens' Website Around North Atlanta

Britney Javens serves buyers and sellers across North Atlanta.

Instead of giving her a single generic page saying she serves "North Atlanta and surrounding areas," we built the website with dedicated content around \*\*eleven individual cities she serves\*\*.

That matters for users.

Someone interested in one community can land on information relevant to that community rather than a generic Atlanta real-estate page.

It also gives search engines substantially more context about the geographic markets associated with the business.

Each community becomes part of the site's structure.

Instead of saying:

\*\*"Britney sells real estate in North Atlanta."\*\*

the website can actually demonstrate what that means.

## Why Dedicated Community Pages Matter

A local landing page shouldn't exist simply to repeat a city name 30 times.

That's not useful.

A good community page should help someone who is genuinely interested in that market.

It can establish:

- where the community is,

- what type of buyer may be interested in it,

- the character of the area,

- housing considerations,

- nearby destinations,

- and a path into available listings.

The exact content depends on the market.

The goal is to create a legitimate destination for someone researching that community.

That's much stronger than duplicating the same paragraph across eleven pages and replacing the city name.

## Karen Hilliard Needed a Different Website

Karen Hilliard also works in North Georgia real estate.

But that doesn't mean her site should simply be a copy of Britney's.

Karen's positioning and markets create different opportunities.

Her website can emphasize areas and property types such as:

\*\*Lake Lanier waterfront real estate\*\*

and:

\*\*Blue Ridge cabins and mountain properties.\*\*

Those are meaningfully different searches.

Someone looking for a waterfront home on Lake Lanier has different priorities than someone looking for a cabin in the North Georgia mountains.

The website should reflect that.

That's one of the biggest advantages of custom development:

\*\*The website can follow the business strategy instead of forcing the business into the website's template.\*\*

## Local SEO Is About More Than Adding City Names

There's an unfortunate version of local SEO that looks like this:

Create 100 pages.

Copy the exact same text onto every page.

Replace "Atlanta" with "Alpharetta."

Replace "Alpharetta" with "Cumming."

Replace "Cumming" with another city.

Publish.

That's not the strategy we're interested in.

A local page should exist because it serves a real purpose for a customer.

If someone lands on a page about Lake Lanier waterfront properties, the page should actually help someone interested in Lake Lanier waterfront properties.

If someone lands on a North Atlanta community page, the content should match that intent.

We're building for the person performing the search first.

Search engines benefit from the resulting clarity.

## Site Speed Is Part of the Experience

One advantage of a carefully built website is control.

We can decide what actually needs to load.

We don't have to install a massive collection of plugins because one tiny feature requires them.

We don't have to keep scripts the business doesn't use.

We don't have to accept every performance decision made by a general-purpose template.

That can produce a leaner site.

And for local businesses, mobile performance is especially important.

Think about how many potential customers first encounter a business.

They're holding a phone.

They search Google.

They tap a result.

The website has a few seconds to establish:

\*\*Yes, you're in the right place.\*\*

A slow or frustrating experience creates unnecessary friction before the business ever gets the opportunity to talk to the prospect.

## What Does a 100/100 SEO Score Actually Mean?

Both realtor builds achieved \*\*100/100 scores on Google's Lighthouse SEO audit\*\* during testing.

That's something we're proud of.

But it's important to explain what that number does—and doesn't—mean.

It does \*\*not\*\* mean Google gives the website a secret 100/100 ranking score.

And it does not guarantee the site will rank first for every target search.

Google Search ranking is far more complicated than a single score.

A strong Lighthouse SEO score tells us the site is satisfying the technical checks Lighthouse evaluates.

That's valuable.

But technical SEO is the foundation, not the entire house.

A site still needs:

- useful content,

- clear structure,

- relevant local information,

- a legitimate business behind it,

- strong reputation signals,

- and time to establish search visibility.

We want both.

\*\*Strong technical foundations and useful content.\*\*

## We Found a Much Bigger Problem Than an SEO Score

One of the most interesting discoveries during these builds had nothing to do with page speed.

It was a button.

A property-search button appeared to be doing exactly what it should.

Click it.

Search listings.

Nothing looked obviously wrong.

But when we traced where the visitor was actually going, we found that the search experience could route the prospect somewhere the realtor didn't want the lead going.

In practical terms:

\*\*A website button intended to help generate leads was capable of sending the opportunity away from the realtor.\*\*

That's a much bigger problem than losing a few points on a website audit.

We fixed the path.

## Your Website Shouldn't Generate Leads for Somebody Else

This sounds ridiculous when stated plainly.

But it's a great example of why we inspect the entire customer journey.

A website isn't successful simply because all the buttons technically work.

We want to know:

\*\*Where does the button go?\*\*

\*\*Who owns that experience?\*\*

\*\*Who receives the lead?\*\*

\*\*Can the prospect easily return?\*\*

\*\*Is the realtor's branding maintained?\*\*

\*\*What happens after the visitor searches for a property?\*\*

The same principle applies outside real estate.

If you're a home-service company, where does your booking button go?

Where does the contact form send information?

Who receives the notification?

Does the lead enter your CRM?

Does anyone follow up?

A website is part of a business process.

We need to test the process, not just the page.

## MLS Search Needed to Work With the Business

Property search is obviously important on a realtor website.

A buyer wants to see homes.

The easiest solution would be to link to any available property-search experience and call the feature finished.

But that's not enough.

For Britney and Karen, the listing-search experience needed to connect prospects with their own real-estate presence rather than quietly sending valuable traffic somewhere else.

That's the difference between:

\*\*having an MLS button\*\*

and:

\*\*thinking through the lead journey.\*\*

Technology should support the agent who paid for the website.

## Google Business Profile Is Part of the Website Strategy

The website isn't an isolated project.

For a local professional, the Google Business Profile and website should support one another.

Someone may discover a realtor by searching the agent's name.

Someone else may encounter the business through Maps or a local result.

Another person may receive the agent's name from a friend and search Google before calling.

In all three situations, the prospect is trying to answer:

\*\*Is this person established and trustworthy?\*\*

That's why we worked on the Google presence alongside the websites.

The website tells one part of the story.

The Google Business Profile tells another.

They should make sense together.

A homeowner choosing a roofer may be making a five-figure decision.

- professional photography,

## Don't Build Pages Just to Have Pages

More pages don't automatically mean more SEO.

We want to know:

\*\*What search intent does this page serve?\*\*

\*\*What question does it answer?\*\*

\*\*What customer is it for?\*\*

\*\*What should the visitor do next?\*\*

If we can't answer those questions, the page probably needs more thought.

That's especially important when building local pages.
