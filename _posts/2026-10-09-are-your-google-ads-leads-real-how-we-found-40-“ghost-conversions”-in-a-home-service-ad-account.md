A Google Ads dashboard can tell you that your business generated 50 conversions last month.

That sounds great.

But here's the question that matters:

\*\*What exactly was a conversion?\*\*

Was it a phone call from a homeowner ready to book a $500 repair?

Was it a submitted service request?

Or did someone accidentally tap your location on Google Maps?

Those actions can look surprisingly similar inside an advertising report if conversion tracking isn't configured correctly.

That's exactly what we found when we audited the Google Ads account for \*\*Appliance Tech Pros\*\*, an appliance repair company in Georgia.

On the surface, the account looked healthy.

Google Ads was reporting dozens of conversions.

The business appeared to be generating a steady flow of leads.

But when we audited what those conversions actually represented, the story changed.

\*\*Nearly 40% of the reported conversions were what we call "ghost conversions"—actions such as map interactions and direction clicks being counted alongside actual leads.\*\*

The company wasn't necessarily getting the lead volume the dashboard suggested.

And that wasn't the only problem.

We also found keywords that hadn't generated a meaningful result in months, irrelevant search terms consuming budget, and campaigns that weren't structured around the types of jobs the company actually wanted.

So we rebuilt the account around a much simpler principle:

\*\*A lead should be a lead.\*\*

Here's what we changed.

## What Is a Google Ads Conversion?

Before talking about what went wrong, it's important to understand what Google Ads means by a "conversion."

A conversion is an action you've told the advertising platform matters to your business.

For a home-service company, legitimate conversion actions might include:

- a qualified phone call,

- a submitted contact form,

- an online booking,

- or another meaningful customer inquiry.

Those are actions that can reasonably turn into revenue.

But accounts can also be configured to count other interactions.

Depending on the setup, things such as location interactions, clicks for directions, or other actions may appear in conversion reporting.

Those actions aren't necessarily worthless.

Someone requesting directions may eventually become a customer.

The problem is treating that action as equivalent to an actual inbound lead.

If your dashboard reports both as "conversions," you may think your advertising is performing much better than it really is.

When Appliance Tech Pros brought us their Google Ads account, the headline numbers looked encouraging.

The reported conversion count suggested the campaigns were producing plenty of activity.

But we didn't want to know how many conversions Google reported.

We wanted to know:

\*\*What happened after someone clicked the ad?\*\*

Once we audited the conversion setup, we discovered that nearly \*\*40% of the reported conversions weren't the kind of leads the owner thought they were paying for.\*\*

They were largely interactions such as accidental map taps and direction clicks being included in the conversion totals.

That's a huge distinction.

Imagine an owner receives a monthly report showing:

\*\*50 conversions\*\*

Naturally, they interpret that as something close to:

\*\*50 leads\*\*

But if a significant portion of those conversions aren't calls, bookings, or genuine inquiries, the owner is making decisions based on an inflated number.

The dashboard isn't necessarily technically incorrect.

It's answering the question it was configured to answer.

The problem is that it may be answering the \*\*wrong business question\*\*.

## Why Bad Conversion Tracking Is So Dangerous

Poor conversion tracking doesn't just make a monthly report look better than it should.

It can affect how advertising decisions are made.

Google Ads relies heavily on conversion data to understand which searches, ads, keywords, and users are valuable.

If the account tells Google:

\*\*"This action is valuable to my business,"\*\*

Google can use that information when optimizing campaigns.

So imagine you're feeding the platform a mixture of:

- real customer phone calls,

- useful inquiries,

- map interactions,

- accidental taps,

- and other low-intent activity.

You're no longer simply measuring bad data.

You may be \*\*optimizing around bad data\*\*.

That's why conversion tracking is one of the first things we look at during a Google Ads audit.

Before asking Google to find more conversions, we need to make sure the conversions we're measuring are actually worth finding.

## For a Home-Service Business, A Phone Call Has Real Value

Home-service businesses have an advantage when measuring advertising performance.

The customer journey is often relatively direct.

Someone has a problem.

They search Google.

They see an ad.

They call.

For an appliance repair company, that search might be:

\*\*"refrigerator repair near me"\*\*

or:

\*\*"washer repair [city]"\*\*

or:

\*\*"oven repair near me"\*\*

If the customer taps the ad and calls the business, that's a meaningful action.

The business has an opportunity to book a job.

That's very different from someone tapping a map listing without ever contacting the company.

So for Appliance Tech Pros, we reworked conversion tracking around the actions that actually mattered.

\*\*Real phone calls from advertising became the focus.\*\*

Now, when the owner looks at performance, the number is much closer to something they actually care about:

\*\*How many opportunities did this advertising create for my business?\*\*

## We Found Keywords That Hadn't Produced a Call in a Year

Conversion tracking wasn't the only issue.

We also reviewed where the advertising budget was actually going.

Some keywords had continued consuming attention or budget despite failing to generate a phone call for an extended period.

In some cases, \*\*a year or more\*\*.

This is where Google Ads management needs to become business management rather than simply campaign management.

A keyword can generate:

- impressions,

- clicks,

- traffic,

- and activity

without generating revenue.

Traffic alone isn't the objective.

A home-service business doesn't need the most website visitors.

It needs the right homeowners calling about services the company actually wants to perform.

If a keyword repeatedly fails to produce those opportunities, we need to ask why we're continuing to pay for it.

## Clicks Are Not Customers

This distinction sounds obvious, but advertising reports can make it easy to forget.

Imagine two keywords.

Keyword A generates 100 clicks.

Keyword B generates 40 clicks.

At first glance, Keyword A looks better.

But then we look at actual calls.

Keyword A produces two qualified calls.

Keyword B produces 12.

Which keyword would the owner rather buy?

That's why we don't want to optimize home-service campaigns around vanity metrics.

We care about what happens \*\*after the click\*\*.

Impressions can be useful.

Clicks can be useful.

Click-through rate can be useful.

But ultimately, a service business needs customers.

The metrics should help us understand how advertising contributes to that outcome.

## Search Terms Matter More Than Most Owners Realize

Another place advertising budgets quietly disappear is the search terms report.

A keyword is what the advertiser targets.

A search term is what the customer actually typed.

Those aren't always the same thing.

Depending on the campaign configuration and keyword matching, Google can show ads for searches that aren't exactly what the advertiser expected.

Some of those variations are valuable.

Others aren't.

For a local service business, irrelevant searches can consume budget without having a realistic chance of becoming a customer.

That's why reviewing search terms is ongoing work.

When we find searches that clearly aren't relevant to the business, they can be excluded with negative keywords or other campaign adjustments.

The goal is simple:

\*\*Stop paying for searches that cannot reasonably become the type of job the company wants.\*\*

## We Blocked the Junk

For Appliance Tech Pros, that meant identifying irrelevant search activity and preventing the account from continuing to spend money there.

One bad click isn't the end of the world.

The problem is repetition.

A few dollars wasted today.

A few more tomorrow.

Then next week.

Then next month.

Across a year, small leaks become real money.

This is one of the reasons we think of Google Ads management as continual refinement rather than a one-time setup.

Customer searches change.

Competition changes.

Services change.

Google's platform changes.

The account needs someone regularly asking:

\*\*"Would we actually want to pay for this search?"\*\*

If the answer is no, it shouldn't quietly continue consuming budget.

## We Restructured Campaigns Around Profitable Job Types

Not every job is equally valuable to a service company.

That's another place generic advertising strategies can fall short.

A home-service company may perform 15 different services.

That doesn't mean it should spend the same amount of advertising money trying to acquire every one of them.

Some job types may have:

- higher average tickets,

- better margins,

- better close rates,

- more available technician capacity,

- or greater strategic value to the business.

Others may be technically services the company provides but not something it wants to aggressively advertise.

We restructured the campaigns around the types of work that made the most sense for the business.

That's an important shift.

Instead of asking:

\*\*"How can we get more Google Ads conversions?"\*\*

we ask:

\*\*"How can we use this budget to generate more of the jobs the business actually wants?"\*\*

Those are not the same question.

## Cost Per Lead Isn't Enough Either

Once conversion tracking is fixed, cost per lead becomes much more meaningful.

But even then, we want to go further.

Suppose Campaign A generates leads for $30 each.

Campaign B generates leads for $60 each.

Campaign A appears to win.

But what if Campaign A mostly produces small, low-margin jobs while Campaign B produces high-value jobs that close at a much higher rate?

The cheaper lead isn't automatically the better lead.

For service businesses, advertising should eventually connect to business economics.

We want to understand:

\*\*What did we spend?\*\*

\*\*How many real opportunities did we generate?\*\*

\*\*What types of jobs did those opportunities represent?\*\*

\*\*Which campaigns produced work the company actually wants?\*\*

That's a much more useful conversation than celebrating a high conversion count.
