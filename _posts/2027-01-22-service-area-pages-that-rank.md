Most home-service companies don't work in one town.

A roofer based in Knoxville runs jobs in Farragut, Maryville, Oak Ridge and Lenoir City. A septic company in North Georgia drives an hour in every direction. An HVAC company covers three counties.

The website, though, usually says one thing:

**"Serving Knoxville and surrounding areas."**

That phrase tells a homeowner in Maryville almost nothing. It tells Google even less.

So contractors hear that they need "city pages." Then someone builds 40 of them in an afternoon, each one a copy of the last with the town name swapped in.

That is not a service area strategy.

**That is a doorway page problem waiting to happen.**

This post covers how to do service area pages the right way: what they're for, what Google says it doesn't want, what a good area page actually contains, and how we've approached them on sites we've built.

## What a Service Area Page Is Supposed to Do

A service area page has one job.

It answers a simple question for someone in a specific place:

**"Does this company actually work here, and do they know this area?"**

That's it. If the page answers that question well, it's useful. If it doesn't, it's filler.

Think about the homeowner on the other end. Their basement is wet after a storm. They search "foundation repair Oak Ridge." They land on your Oak Ridge page.

What do they need to see?

- That you serve Oak Ridge, clearly, near the top.
- The services you offer there.
- Some sign that you've actually worked in that area.
- How to call you, fast.

Good area pages are built around that visitor. Bad ones are built around a keyword list.

## What Google Means by "Doorway Pages"

Google's spam policies specifically name doorway pages as something it works against.

In plain English, doorway pages are pages created mainly to rank for a set of very similar searches, where each page doesn't offer anything meaningfully different. Google's own examples include having many pages targeting different cities or regions that funnel visitors to the same place.

That description fits a lot of contractor websites.

You've probably seen them:

- "Roof Repair in Maryville, TN"
- "Roof Repair in Alcoa, TN"
- "Roof Repair in Lenoir City, TN"

Open any two of them side by side and the only differences are the town name and maybe the ZIP code. Same paragraphs. Same photos. Same "we are proud to serve the residents of [CITY]."

Google is very good at recognizing that pattern.

Pages like that may not help, and at scale they can make the whole site look lower quality. We'd rather not take that risk with a client's website.

**The test is simple: if you could swap the city name and the page would be just as true, it's not a real area page.**

## The Difference Between Thin and Useful

Here is how we think about it.

### A thin area page

- A heading with the city name.
- Two or three generic paragraphs.
- A list of services copied from the homepage.
- A phone number.
- Nothing that could only be written about that place.

### A useful area page

- Clear confirmation that you serve that town and nearby communities.
- Services you actually offer there (which may not be every service you offer everywhere).
- Local context that matters to the work.
- Real projects, photos or job notes from that area, if you have them.
- Answers to questions people in that area actually ask.
- A clear way to call or request an estimate.

The second version takes more work.

That's the point. The effort is what makes it different from the 40 pages a competitor generated in an afternoon.

## What "Local Content" Actually Means for a Contractor

People hear "unique local content" and assume it means writing about the town's history or listing restaurants.

That's not useful to someone with a roof leak.

Local content for a home-service business should be about **how the work is different in that place.** A few examples of the kinds of things that can make an area page genuinely local:

### Terrain and soil

A septic or grading company knows that some parts of its service area are rocky, some are steep, and some hold water. That changes what kind of system gets installed, how long a job takes, and what a homeowner should expect. Writing that down, honestly and in your own words, is local content.

### Housing stock

An older neighborhood with homes built decades ago has different plumbing, electrical and roofing issues than a newer subdivision. A remodeler or plumber who works in both knows the difference. Say so.

### Lakes, hills and access

Around Knoxville, a lot of homes sit near the lakes or up on ridges. Long driveways, steep lots and limited access affect how equipment gets in. If that matters to your work, it belongs on the page.

### Permits and local requirements

If the county or city handles permits or inspections differently, a short, accurate note helps the homeowner. Only include what you know to be true and current. If you're not sure, say "we handle the permitting" rather than guessing at the rules.

### Seasonal patterns

If storms, freezes or summer heat create predictable work in a particular area, that's worth explaining.

None of this requires invented statistics or fluff. It requires the knowledge you and your crews already have from working there.

## Real Projects Are the Strongest Local Signal

The single best thing you can put on an area page is proof that you've worked there.

That might be:

- A short write-up of a job: what the problem was, what you did, how it turned out.
- Photos from the job site (with the homeowner's permission, and without showing their house number if they prefer).
- A review from a customer in that area, pulled from your real Google reviews.

A homeowner in Lenoir City who sees a roof you replaced two streets over doesn't need to be convinced you serve Lenoir City.

You've shown them.

This is also why area pages work best as **living pages**. When you finish a notable job in a town, add it. Over time the page gets more specific and more useful, which is the opposite of a copy-paste page that never changes.

## How Many Area Pages Should You Have?

Fewer than you think, at first.

Start with the towns where:

1. You actually do a meaningful amount of work.
2. You want more work.
3. You have something real to say.

If you can only write a genuinely useful page for six towns, build six. You can add more as you build up projects and knowledge in other areas.

A site with six strong area pages is in a better position than a site with 60 interchangeable ones.

### Match your Google Business Profile

Your area pages should line up with the service areas listed on your Google Business Profile. If your profile says you serve Maryville and Alcoa, your website should say so too.

## A Simple Structure for a Contractor Area Page

Here's a structure we like. Adjust it to the business.

1. **Heading that names the service and the town.** For example: "Septic Pumping and Repair in Maryville, TN."
2. **A short opening paragraph** confirming you serve the town and nearby communities, written naturally.
3. **Tap-to-call button and estimate form** near the top, especially on mobile.
4. **Services offered in this area**, each linking to its main service page.
5. **What's different about working here**: terrain, housing, access, seasonal issues.
6. **Recent work in the area**: photos, short job notes, real reviews.
7. **Nearby communities you also serve**, linking to their pages if they exist.
8. **A short FAQ** with questions people in that area actually ask.
9. **A closing call to action.**

### Link it into the site properly

Area pages shouldn't float on their own. Link to them from:

- Your main service pages ("We also serve Maryville, Alcoa and Lenoir City").
- A service areas page or section on the homepage.
- Your footer, if the list is short.

And link from each area page back to the main service pages. That internal linking helps visitors move around and helps search engines understand how your pages relate.

## The Technical Side

A few details that matter on hand-built sites:

### Title tags and descriptions

Each area page needs its own title tag and meta description written for that town. Not a template that produces the same sentence with a swapped city name.

### Structured data

Your business schema can list the areas you serve using the `areaServed` property, and each page can carry breadcrumbs so search engines understand where it sits in the site. On our own site, we use ProfessionalService business schema with our service areas listed, plus breadcrumbs on every page, and it validated with zero errors in Google's Rich Results Test and the Schema.org validator. We explained how that works in [Schema Markup for Local Service Businesses, Explained in Plain English](/blog/schema-markup-for-local-service-businesses/).

### Don't fake a location

An area page says "we serve this town." It should never imply you have an office there if you don't. No fake addresses, no virtual offices listed as locations. That's misleading to customers and it's against Google's guidelines for business profiles.

### Keep the pages fast

Area pages are often the pages people land on from a phone. They should load quickly and put the phone number within reach of a thumb.

## Common Mistakes We See

- **Building pages for towns you rarely serve.** If you won't drive there, don't build the page.
- **Copying the homepage text onto every area page.** That's duplication, not local content.
- **Stuffing the town name into every sentence.** Write naturally. Mention it where it makes sense.
- **Auto-generated "nearby landmarks" sections.** Nobody hiring a plumber cares about the nearest museum.
- **Never updating them.** A page that hasn't changed since launch misses the chance to show recent work.
- **Orphan pages.** If nothing on your site links to the area page, visitors won't find it and search engines may not treat it as important.

## How Long Before Area Pages Help?

Honestly, it takes time.

Organic SEO usually takes months, and roughly six months is a fair expectation before you can judge whether a set of pages is working. New pages need to be found, indexed and tested by Google against everything else out there.

Rankings tend to move before calls do. You may see your area pages start appearing in Search Console for searches in those towns before anyone clicks. That's normal. We'll cover how to read those signals in an upcoming post on what our own Search Console data taught us.

Nobody can promise that an area page will rank in a specific position. What you can do is build pages that deserve to.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are city pages bad for SEO?

Not by themselves. Copy-paste city pages that only swap the town name are the problem. Area pages with real local information, real projects and a clear purpose can be genuinely useful to customers.

### Can I rank in a town where I don't have an office?

Your website can explain that you serve a town, and your Google Business Profile can list it as a service area if you're a service-area business. Whether you appear in the map results for that town depends on many factors Google weighs, including distance. Your organic website pages can sometimes reach further than the map results.

### How long should an area page be?

Long enough to be useful, and no longer. Some will be short because you have less to say. Others will grow as you add projects. Don't pad a page with filler to hit a word count.

### Should every service have its own page in every town?

Usually not. A page for every service and every town multiplies quickly into hundreds of thin pages. Most contractors are better served by strong main service pages plus one solid page per important town.

## Build Area Pages You'd Be Proud to Show a Customer

Here's the simplest way to judge an area page.

Pull it up on your phone. Imagine handing it to a homeowner in that town.

Would they feel like you know their area? Would they know how to reach you? Would they see work you've done nearby?

If yes, it's a real page.

If it reads like a form letter with their town name pasted in, it's not.
