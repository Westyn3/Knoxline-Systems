Every home-service owner has had this conversation.

"We need more Google reviews."

Everyone agrees. The techs nod. The office manager says she'll start asking.

Then a busy week hits. A septic pump-out runs long, an AC call turns into a compressor replacement, a roof leak comes in at 4:45 on a Friday.

Nobody asks.

Three months later, the same conversation happens again.

That is not a people problem. It is a process problem.

The companies with hundreds of recent, real reviews usually aren't doing anything magical. They decided that asking for a review is part of finishing a job, the same way collecting payment is part of finishing a job. Then they built a system so it happens whether anyone remembers or not.

This post walks through how we build that system for home-service businesses: when to ask, how to make it easy, how to follow up, how to reply, and the Google rules you cannot break along the way.

## Why Reviews Matter So Much for Home-Service Companies

When a homeowner searches for a plumber or a septic company, they are usually choosing between strangers.

They have never met you. They don't know your techs. They can't see your truck in their neighbor's driveway.

What they can see is your Google Business Profile. Your star rating. Your review count. How recent those reviews are. Whether you reply.

**Reviews are the closest thing to word of mouth that a stranger can access.**

They also matter to Google. Google says local results are based on three main factors: relevance, distance and prominence. Prominence is about how well known and trusted a business is, and Google states that review count and rating are part of that picture.

So reviews do two jobs at once:

- They help a customer decide to call you instead of the next listing.
- They are one of the signals Google uses to understand how established your business is.

No review strategy can guarantee a ranking. But a steady flow of real reviews is one of the few things in local SEO that you control almost entirely.

## What a Review System Actually Looks Like

When we say "review system," we mean a repeatable process with five parts:

1. **A trigger:** the moment a job is done and the customer is happy.
2. **A direct link:** one tap takes the customer straight to the review form.
3. **An in-person ask:** the tech or owner asks face to face, with a card or QR code to make it easy.
4. **A follow-up:** a text or email goes out automatically, with the same link.
5. **A reply:** every review gets a response from the business.

None of these steps is complicated on its own. The value comes from doing all five, every job, without relying on memory.

## Step 1: Get Your Direct Review Link

The single biggest barrier to getting reviews is friction.

If you tell a customer "look us up on Google and leave a review," you are asking them to search, find the right listing, scroll, find the review button, and sign in. Plenty of happy customers will mean to do it and never get around to it.

A direct review link removes most of that.

Google Business Profile gives you a shareable link that opens the review form for your business. You can find it from your profile's management options in Google Search or Maps (look for the option to ask for or share reviews). The exact menu location changes from time to time, but the link is there.

Once you have it:

- Save it somewhere the whole team can access.
- Shorten it or turn it into a QR code.
- Use the exact same link everywhere: cards, texts, emails, invoices.

That one link becomes the foundation of the whole system.

## Step 2: Ask at the Right Moment

Timing matters more than wording.

The best time to ask for a review is when the customer feels the relief of a solved problem. For most home-service work, that is right at the end of the job:

- The septic tank is pumped and the backup is gone.
- The AC is blowing cold again on a July afternoon.
- The roof is patched before the next storm.
- The dryer works and the laundry pile can finally shrink.

At that moment the customer is grateful and the experience is fresh.

Wait two weeks and that feeling fades. The job becomes "that thing we paid for." The review request becomes one more message in a crowded inbox.

### Who Should Ask?

Usually the person the customer just dealt with. That's the tech in the driveway or the owner who walked the job.

A simple, honest ask works:

> "If you were happy with how this went, a Google review really helps a small company like ours. I'll leave you a card with a QR code, and you'll get a text with the link too."

That's it. No script needs to be longer than that.

### What About Big Jobs?

For longer projects like a roof replacement, a septic install or a grading job, the right moment might be the final walkthrough or the day the final invoice is paid. Pick a clear milestone so the team knows exactly when to ask.

## Step 3: Use QR Code Review Cards

This is one of the simplest tools we set up for clients, and one of the most useful.

A review card is a small printed card, usually business-card size, with:

- Your logo and company name.
- A short line like "How did we do?"
- A QR code that opens your direct review link.
- The short link printed underneath for people who don't scan codes.

The tech hands it over at the end of the job. The customer can scan it on the spot or stick it on the fridge for later.

A few practical notes:

- **Keep cards in every truck.** If the cards live in the office, they don't get handed out.
- **Test the QR code** before printing a thousand of them. Scan it on more than one phone.
- **Put the QR code on invoices and leave-behinds too.** Every touchpoint can carry the same link.

The card does something subtle. It turns "please review us" from a vague request into a physical reminder that sits in the customer's kitchen.

## Step 4: Automate the Follow-Up Text

The in-person ask is good. The in-person ask plus an automatic follow-up is much better.

Most home-service companies already run their jobs through some kind of software: a CRM, a field service app, a scheduling tool or an invoicing system. That software already knows when a job is finished.

That event can trigger a review request.

For example:

- Job is marked complete in the CRM, then a text goes out with the review link.
- Invoice is paid, then an email goes out thanking the customer and including the link.
- If there's no review after a few days, one polite reminder goes out. Then it stops.

This is where our automation work and our marketing work overlap. We've written before about [what business process automation is](/blog/what-is-business-process-automation-and-do-you-need-it/), and review requests are one of the cleanest examples. The data already exists. You're just using it to make the next step happen consistently.

### Keep the Message Short

A follow-up text should be easy to read in two seconds:

> "Thanks for choosing [Company] today. If you have a minute, a Google review helps us a lot: [link]"

Use your real company name. Send it from a number the customer will recognize. Don't send five reminders. One ask and one reminder is plenty.

## Step 5: Reply to Every Review

Replying is the part most businesses skip, and it is one of the easiest wins.

Google lets business owners respond publicly to reviews on their profile. Those replies are visible to everyone who reads your reviews, which means you are really writing for the next customer, not just the one who left the review.

### Replying to Positive Reviews

Keep it short, specific and human:

- Thank them by name if they used it.
- Mention the service if it fits naturally ("Glad we could get the drain field sorted out before the holidays").
- Don't paste the same canned line on every review.

It's fine to mention the service or the town in a reply when it's natural. It is not fine to stuff every reply with keywords. Write like a person.

### Replying to Negative Reviews

Every company that does enough work eventually gets a bad review. How you respond matters more than the review itself.

- **Don't argue in public.** The next customer is watching.
- **Acknowledge the experience** without admitting things that aren't true.
- **Take it offline.** Offer a direct phone number or name to call.
- **Stay calm and brief.** Two or three sentences is usually enough.

A thoughtful reply to a one-star review can actually build trust. It shows a homeowner that if something goes wrong, the company will deal with it like adults.

### What About Fake or Off-Topic Reviews?

If a review clearly violates Google's policies (spam, a review meant for a different business, offensive content), you can flag it for Google to evaluate. Google decides whether to remove it. There's no guarantee, so don't count on removals as a strategy.

## The Rules: What Google Does Not Allow

This section matters more than any tactic above.

Google has clear policies about reviews, and breaking them can lead to reviews being removed or your profile being restricted. Some of these tactics are still sold by marketing companies. Don't buy them.

### Never Buy Reviews

Paying anyone to write reviews, whether a service, a freelancer or "a guy who knows a guy," is fake engagement. Google prohibits it. It is also the fastest way to put your profile at risk.

### Don't Offer Incentives

Offering a discount, gift card, raffle entry or free service in exchange for a review is not allowed under Google's policies. A sincere "thank you" is fine. A coupon for a five-star review is not.

### Don't Gate Reviews

Review gating means screening customers first ("How would you rate us, 1 to 5?") and only sending the Google link to the happy ones while routing unhappy ones somewhere else.

Google does not allow selectively soliciting positive reviews. Ask every customer the same way. Yes, that means some customers who had a bad day will leave honest feedback. That's part of having a real profile.

### No Review Swaps

Trading reviews with another business ("I'll review your landscaping company if you review my roofing company") is a conflict of interest. So is having employees, family members or the owner review their own business. These are prohibited.

### Don't Write Reviews for Customers

Don't draft a review and ask a customer to post it. Don't post reviews on a customer's behalf. Every review should be written by the person who had the experience, in their own words.

**The honest version of this system is also the only one that lasts.**

## Common Mistakes That Stall Review Growth

A few patterns show up again and again:

- **Asking only when the owner remembers.** Reviews spike after a staff meeting, then go quiet for months.
- **Sending customers to search for you.** No direct link means lots of lost reviews.
- **Waiting too long.** A request three weeks after the job feels like an afterthought.
- **Never replying.** A profile full of unanswered reviews looks unattended.
- **One big push, then nothing.** Fifty reviews in one month and none for the next six looks odd to customers reading your profile.
- **Cutting corners on policy.** Incentives, gating and swaps can cost you far more than they gain.

The fix for almost all of these is the same: make the ask automatic and consistent.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many Google reviews does a home-service business need?

There's no magic number. What matters is how you compare to the other businesses showing up for the same searches in your area, and whether your reviews are recent. A steady flow every month is more useful than a big batch once a year.

### Can I text customers asking for a Google review?

Yes. Sending your direct review link by text or email after a job is a normal, allowed practice. What's not allowed is offering something in exchange or only asking customers you expect to be happy. Make sure customers have agreed to receive texts from your business.

### Should I respond to every Google review?

We recommend it. Short, genuine replies to positive reviews and calm, brief replies to negative ones show future customers that a real person runs the business and pays attention.

### Can I get a bad review removed?

You can flag a review that violates Google's policies, and Google will evaluate it. Google doesn't remove reviews just because they're negative. The best long-term answer to a bad review is a professional reply and a steady stream of real, positive ones.

### Is it okay to ask family and employees for reviews?

No. Reviews from owners, employees or people with a conflict of interest violate Google's policies. Reviews should come from real customers describing real experiences.
